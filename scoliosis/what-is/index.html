What is scoliosis?

Scoliosis is a sideways curvature of the spine, measured on X-ray. A curve of 10° or more (the “Cobb angle”) counts as scoliosis; below that is usually called spinal asymmetry. Here's what that means in practice.

The one-sentence version

Viewed from behind, a healthy spine runs straight; with scoliosis, it curves sideways — often with a slight rotation of the vertebrae — most often in the mid-back (thoracic) or lower back (lumbar), and usually in a single long “C” or a double “S” pattern.

The main types

TypeTypical ageWhat it means
Adolescent idiopathic (AIS)10–18The most common form (~80% of cases). “Idiopathic” = no known single cause; genetics play a role. Most AIS curves are mild and never need more than monitoring.
Adult degenerative (“de novo”)50+Develops later in life as discs and joints wear unevenly. Very common — one study found thoracic scoliosis in about a quarter of people over 50, usually mild (10°–20°) [1].
Adult progression of AIS30+Childhood curves that persist or slowly worsen into adulthood, sometimes accelerating after menopause.
Functional (non-structural)AnyThe curve comes from something outside the spine — commonly a leg-length difference or muscle guarding — and often improves when the cause is addressed.
Congenital / neuromuscular / syndromicBirth–childhoodRarer forms from vertebrae forming abnormally, or conditions like cerebral palsy or muscular dystrophy. Managed by specialist teams.

How common is it?

  • About 2–3% of the US population — roughly 6–9 million people — has scoliosis.
  • Only a small fraction of those curves ever need treatment: most are mild and simply observed.
  • In older adults the numbers rise sharply: degenerative curves become common from the 50s onward [1] — scoliosis is not just a teenage condition.

The Cobb angle, in one paragraph

On a standing X-ray, the radiologist tilts the most-tilted vertebra at the top of the curve and the most-tilted one at the bottom; the angle where those two lines cross is the Cobb angle. It's the universal yardstick: 10°+ = scoliosis; ~10–25° is mild (usually monitor every 4–6 months in growing teens); ~25–45° moderate (bracing may be discussed in growing teens; exercise therapy often prescribed); 45–50°+ severe (surgery may be discussed, mainly when progressing or painful). Two doctors measuring the same film can differ by a few degrees — that's normal, and one reason a single number shouldn't drive panic.

Measuring your own X-ray? Apps and tools that measure the Cobb angle from an uploaded X-ray are regulated as medical devices in the US for good reason: wrong measurements lead to wrong decisions. Bring questions to your provider instead — ask for the angle, the direction, and the plan for monitoring.

When to see a doctor promptly

  • New or worsening numbness, tingling, or weakness in legs or arms
  • Difficulty breathing or chest tightness linked to your spine
  • Rapidly visible curve change, rib hump growth, or clothes fitting unevenly
  • Back pain that wakes you at night, or with fever/unexplained weight loss
  • Any curve discovered during growth spurts (get a baseline X-ray)

For stable mild curves in adults, the usual path is periodic monitoring, exercise, and pain management — not surgery.
