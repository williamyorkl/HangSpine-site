Exercises and scoliosis: what the evidence supports

Two honest rules up front. First: general exercises are for mobility, core control and confidence — none of them is proven to straighten a curve by itself. Second: the strongest exercise evidence in scoliosis belongs to scoliosis-specific programs (Schroth and the PSSE family) delivered by trained clinicians. Everything below is labeled accordingly.

Start here: scoliosis-specific exercise (PSSE / Schroth)

Best evidence

Physiotherapeutic Scoliosis-Specific Exercises — the Schroth method and its relatives — are individually prescribed, curve-pattern-aware programs combining elongation, de-rotation and side-shift with specialized breathing. International conservative-care guidelines (SOSORT) position PSSE as first-line conservative management, and supervised programs have shown meaningful outcomes in studies, including a 12-month study of braced adolescents where a Schroth program significantly improved curve severity and quality of life [1].

Practical takeaway: if your curve is moderate or progressing, the highest-value move you can make is finding a Schroth/PSSE-trained physical therapist (search the SOSORT society or BSPTS/Schroth provider directories), not assembling exercises from the internet.

General spine-friendly movements

These four appear constantly in physical-therapy programs for backs of all kinds. For people with scoliosis they're usually safe adjuncts — mobility, core endurance, body awareness — with the caveat that they are not curve-specific corrections.

Mobility · widely used

Cat-Cow (cat-camel)

Segmental mobility through the whole spine, plus a gentle way to learn moving one vertebra at a time — a skill Schroth programs also build on.

  1. On hands and knees, wrists under shoulders, knees under hips.
  2. Exhale and round the spine upward (cat), letting the head drop.
  3. Inhale and let the chest sink while the sit bones lift (cow).
  4. Move slowly, 8–12 cycles, trying to feel each segment in turn.
Core stability · widely used

Bird-Dog

Trains contralateral (opposite arm-leg) control and lumbar stability while limiting spine load — a staple in low-back rehabilitation research.

  1. From hands and knees, brace the abdomen lightly.
  2. Extend one arm forward and the opposite leg back, hips level.
  3. Hold 3–5 seconds; avoid tipping or arching the low back.
  4. 6–8 reps per side, quality over height.
Core stability · widely used

Dead Bug

Anti-extension core control with the back safely supported on the floor — a good starting point when bird-dog feels unstable.

  1. Lie on your back, arms up, hips and knees at 90°.
  2. Press the low back gently into the floor.
  3. Lower opposite arm and leg toward the floor without letting the back arch.
  4. Return and alternate; 6–8 per side, slow tempo.
Some scoliosis-specific research

Side Plank

The one general exercise with a (small, preliminary) scoliosis literature: case-series work by Fishman and colleagues used a single-sided yoga side plank in mild idiopathic scoliosis and reported curve improvement in many patients. Important caveats: small studies, no control groups, and side selection must match the curve direction — which is exactly why this one belongs with a clinician, not self-prescribed. Where dead hangs fit

Adjunct · limited direct evidence

Hanging gives temporary spinal decompression, a lat/shoulder stretch, grip work, and — for many people — a habit that's easy to keep. Direct evidence that hanging changes a scoliosis curve doesn't exist yet; a 2024 study of hanging exercise combined with muscle-energy techniques reported short-term improvements in adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis, but that's a beginning, not a verdict. How to use this page

  • Pain-free, doctor-cleared, curve aware: these are adjuncts, not treatments.
  • If an exercise increases asymmetry sensations or pain for more than a day, stop and ask a PT.
  • Consistency beats intensity: 10 focused minutes most days beats one heroic session a week.
