Scoliosis, explained without the hype

Scoliosis affects an estimated 6–9 million people in the United States. Most online content about it swings between “nothing works” and “this one stretch fixes it.” This guide takes the middle road: what scoliosis is, what the evidence actually supports, and where simple habits like dead hangs fit.

Who this is for

You might be an adult who just learned your back asymmetry has a name. A parent reading after your child's screening letter. Or someone over 50 with new low-back pain and a “de novo” curve on an X-ray report. All three of you are common — scoliosis isn't only a teenage condition, and an estimated quarter of people over 50 have at least a mild thoracic curve (Urrutia et al., 2014).

What this guide will consistently not tell you: that any app, stretch, or gadget will straighten your spine on its own. Curve management is clinician territory. What we will do is explain the vocabulary, the evidence, and the low-barrier habits — like hanging — that many people use alongside proper care.

Our editorial rules

  • No miracle language. “Fixes”, “cures”, “corrects” — you won't see them here.
