FREQUENCY GUIDE · 7 MIN READ

How often should you dead hang?

The most common question in hanging communities gets the widest range of answers: "every day!" says one thread, "twice a week, never to failure" says another. Both are right — for different goals. Here's the frequency table by goal and experience level, the reasoning behind it, and the one rule that prevents most elbow niggles.

By William, developer of HangSpine · Sources at the end · Nov 8, 2026
TL;DR

3–6 easy sessions a week, 1–3 total minutes each, holds of 15–40 seconds covers nearly everyone. Daily is fine for relaxed decompression hangs — the pattern that hurts people isn't frequency, it's frequent max attempts. True max tests: once or twice a month. Deload week: every 6–8 weeks (forearm tendons adapt on a slower clock than muscles — the reason behind half the advice on this page). Read the table as a starting prescription, not a law: any row beats no row, and the "established" versions assume months of consistent work with zero symptoms. The dose that matters for most people is the first one — easy frequency, modest holds, no testing — because its compliance cost is nearly zero and its effect compounds daily.

Why easy-daily beats heroic-weekly

The decompression and mobility benefits of hanging are temporary by design — the spine re-compresses the moment you sit back down (that's physics, not a flaw; we walk through the mechanism in the evidence review). A benefit that fades in hours argues for frequency, not intensity: four 30-second hangs across a workday keep re-visiting the range, while one heroic 3-minute set on Sunday only helps Sunday. Grip strength plays the opposite game — it cares about total weekly volume and progressive overload, which is why the strength rows in the table hold session counts steady and let volume grow through the planner instead.

There's also a behavioral half: frequency is the habit's heartbeat. Community longevity stories — people hanging daily for years — almost universally describe small, unremarkable sessions anchored to daily life: after coffee, before the shower, between meetings (our desk-worker protocol is built entirely on that logic). Nobody's year-three story starts with "I trained to failure twice a week."

The tendon clock: the rule that prevents elbow niggles

The reason every cautious coach caps frequency isn't the muscles — forearm flexors recover in a day. It's the connective tissue: grip tendons and shoulder structures adapt over weeks-to-months, and they fail quietly. The practical translation is three rules:

  1. First hang of every session at half effort — the cold-tendon opener is where niggle history begins (full routine: warm-up checklist).
  2. Max tests are a monthly event, not a daily sport — a max attempt is a measurement, and measurements don't need a weekly cadence to be useful; trends matter more than snapshots.
  3. Every 6–8 weeks, one lighter week — roughly half the volume, no tests. Boring on purpose; it's when the tendons catch up to the muscles. The planner builds this in automatically.

Ignore all three and the bill arrives as a dull inner-elbow ache two to three weeks after your volume jumped — the classic delayed invoice of hanging too hard too soon. The repair is duller: halve volume for a week, keep frequency, return slowly.

What "easy" should feel like

  • Conversational effort — you could speak a full sentence at second 25 of a 30-second hold.
  • Done before failure — the set ends when form quality dips, not when fingers peel. Failure is for the monthly test only.
  • Zero next-day cost — morning-after forearm ache that fades by lunch is adaptation; anything that alters your grip on the steering wheel is volume debt.
  • Boring is the brand — the sessions you don't remember doing are the ones building the base.

Fitting hangs around other training

The most common real-world collision is hanging versus pulling work — rows, pull-ups, climbs all tax the same forearm flexors. The rule of thumb: hangs before pulls on the same day, never as an afterthought to a fried grip. If your pull session leaves the forearms spent, your hang "volume" that day is fiction anyway — count it as rest, not as a missed dose. Lifters running the progression planner should treat planner sessions as the anchor and workday decompression hangs as free extras that don't count against the plan. Climbers are the special case: hanging is sport-specific training, so the frequency table's climbing-prep row replaces, rather than adds to, some fingerboard volume — the two compete for the same tendons, and tendons invoice both.

One more integration note: hanging pairs oddly well with everything precisely because it demands so little. It doesn't compete for recovery the way a second lifting day would, it doesn't need equipment beyond a bar, and a 30-second hold doesn't require changing clothes. That's the whole reason the frequency table can recommend "daily" for a decompression goal without blinking — the dose is small enough that daily is sustainable, and sustainable is the entire game. If you remember one sentence from this page: the best frequency is the one that survives your worst week. Size your hangs so even a disaster Tuesday still fits them in.

The fixes are unglamorous: too much → cut volume in half, keep frequency, add a deload; too little → attach hangs to an existing daily anchor and drop the session length until it's embarrassingly easy. Frequency problems are almost never solved by willpower — they're solved by resizing the dose.

FAQ

How often should you dead hang?

3–6 easy sessions a week, 1–3 total minutes each, holds of 15–40 seconds. Daily relaxed hangs are fine; frequent max attempts are the actual hazard.

Can you dead hang every day?

Yes, at easy effort — most long-term hangers do. Monthly max tests and a deload every 6–8 weeks keep the tendons ahead of the load.

How long per set?

15–40 seconds for volume work, well before failure. Long 60–90s endurance holds are their own skill, trained in dedicated sets.

Daily short or weekly long?

For decompression, daily short — the benefit is temporary and frequency refreshes it. For grip, total weekly volume rules. Pick what you'll sustain for years.
