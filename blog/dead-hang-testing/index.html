How to Test Your Dead Hang Without Wasting a Training Day

A max hang test should cost five minutes, not a whole session. Here is the protocol that gives you a usable number without wrecking the rest of your training week.

By William, developer of HangSpine · Sources at the end · Nov 25, 2026
TL;DR

Warm up for 90 seconds, do one easy 10-second hang, rest 2–3 minutes, then take one honest max attempt and stop when the grip opens. Repeat every 4–6 weeks under the same conditions. Don’t test daily, don’t test cold, and don’t test after a maximal climbing or pull-up session.

Why a max test can cost you a session

A true max hang is not a warm-up. It is a high-effort grip event that leaves the forearms, fingers and shoulders fatigued for hours. If you test at the start of a climbing session, your first hard boulder feels worse than it should; if you test before pull-ups, your rep count drops. That is why “test day” and “training day” often conflict.

The fix is not to avoid testing. It is to budget it: test when the number matters, keep the test short, and do not stack it on top of the hardest grip work of the week. One honest test every 4–6 weeks is enough for a trend.

When to test

  • Every 4–6 weeks — often at the end of a block or after a deload week.
  • Same time of day, same bar, same grip — otherwise you are measuring the setup.
  • Not after a hard climbing or pull-up session — wait at least 24–48 hours, or test at the end of an easy day.
  • Not when sleep is terrible or you are sick — you will get a number, but it will not be a useful trend point.

Test vs. training: the 24-hour rule

A max hang is a training stimulus as well as a measurement. If you test on Monday, do not schedule a hard climbing or pull-up session that evening and expect the same output. A simple rule: the test is the hardest grip thing you do that day. If you want to train afterwards, keep it to rows, legs or easy technique work; leave the hard grip work for another day. The 24-hour rule is not magic, but it prevents the common mistake of turning a five-minute test into a wasted training week.

If the test is not the priority and you are already in a session, put it at the end — after your hard climbing or pull-up work — and accept that the number will be lower. Just label it “post-session” and do not compare it with a fresh test.

If you only have a doorway bar

That is enough. Use a bar you can grip fully, at a height you can reach without jumping. Check the frame and the bar rating before every test; a doorway bar that shifts mid-hang is not a test condition, it is a safety problem. Warm up the same way, use the same grip, and start the clock when the feet leave the floor. A doorway bar is a valid setup as long as it is stable and repeatable.

If the bar is too low to hang freely, use a chair to get into position and then take your feet off; do not push off the chair. If the bar is too high to reach safely, use a step and a spotter. A test is not worth a fall.

Hang test vs. grip strength test

They answer different questions. A dead hang measures time under bodyweight tension; a dynamometer measures peak force. A hang is more specific to climbing and hanging performance; a dynamometer is better for tracking the health-associated grip marker. If you have both, record both but do not expect them to move in lockstep. Our grip age calculator covers the dynamometer side; this protocol is for the hang.

A simple 4-week test block

Week 1: test and record the baseline. Weeks 2–3: train normally with submaximal hangs and rows; no max tests. Week 4: deload for 3–4 days, then repeat the test under the same conditions. That gives you one clean comparison per month without turning every session into a measurement. If you want a structured plan, the progression planner does the arithmetic.

A good test day is boring: normal sleep, normal food, no alcohol the night before, and the same time of day as last time. Do not add a new pre-workout, a new bar or a new grip on test day; those are variables, not preparation. The goal is a number you can compare, not a performance you can brag about.

If the test goes badly, do not immediately re-test to “fix” it. A bad day is data too: note sleep, stress and soreness, and keep the next test on schedule. Re-testing until you get a good number is how a benchmark becomes a mood ring.

The 5-minute test protocol

  1. 0:00–1:30 — warm up. Arm circles, scapular pulls, and one easy 10-second hang. Our 90-second warm-up checklist is free.
  2. 1:30–2:30 — rest. Shake out the arms, breathe, and set your timer. No max effort yet.
  3. 2:30–3:00 — the attempt. Full grip, feet off the floor, shoulders relaxed. Start the clock when the feet leave the floor; stop when the grip opens or the shoulders shrug up uncontrollably.
  4. 3:00–5:00 — optional second attempt. Rest 3–5 minutes and repeat once if you want a best-of-two. More than two attempts is fatigue testing, not performance testing.
  5. Log it. Record the time, bar, grip, bodyweight, sleep and RPE. A number without conditions is not a benchmark.

If you use a watch or app, make sure it starts and stops consistently. A hand-timed test with a friend is fine if you use the same start/stop rule every time.

How to read the result

Treat a change of less than about 10% as noise. A 25-second hang that becomes 27 seconds is not necessarily progress; a 25 that becomes 32 is a signal. Compare with the benchmarks guide for context, but keep your own trend as the primary metric.

If the number is flat for two consecutive tests, do not test more. Change one input — volume, assistance, or recovery — and re-test after another 4–6 weeks. Our plateau guide walks through that decision.

What to do next

Set one target 10–15 seconds above the current number, train for 4–6 weeks, then repeat this test. If you want a written block, the progression planner turns the number into a plan; the calculator gives you a quick benchmark by age and sex.

Do not chase a personal best every week. The test is a camera, not a training method.

Common mistakes

  • Testing cold — a lower number that says nothing about your training.
  • Testing daily — turns a benchmark into a fatigue test.
  • Testing after hard grip work — measures residual fatigue, not capacity.
  • Changing the setup — a different bar or grip is a different test.
  • Stopping too late — holding past “grip open” adds noise and injury risk.

FAQ

How often should I test my dead hang?

Every 4–6 weeks, not every session. A true max test is a high-effort event; testing more often measures fatigue, not progress.

Should I test before or after my workout?

After a thorough warm-up and before hard grip work if the test is the priority; otherwise at the end of an easy session — never after a maximal climbing or pull-up session.

How many attempts should I take?

One honest attempt is enough. A second after 3–5 minutes is optional for a best-of-two; more than that is fatigue testing.

Why does my hang time change so much?

Grip, bar diameter, warm-up, sleep, bodyweight and start/stop rules all move the number. Use the same conditions and a ~10% noise band.

Do I need a special bar?

No. Use a stable bar you can grip fully and reach without jumping. Keep the same bar for retests when possible.
