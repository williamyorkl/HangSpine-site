How often should you dead hang?

Short answer: 3–5 sessions a week is a robust default, daily easy hangs are fine, and twice a week is a reasonable floor for grip. The longer answer is that "how often" is really three dials — sessions, holds per session, and how close to max — and most people turn the wrong one.

By William, developer of HangSpine · Sources at the end · Nov 8, 2026
TL;DR

Habit: 1–2 easy holds most days, 2–4 minutes total. General grip/strength: 3 sessions a week, 3–5 holds of 15–30 seconds. First pull-up: 3 sessions a week with hangs plus scapular pulls. Experienced: 3–4 sessions, 4–6 holds of 20–40 seconds, deload every 4–6 weeks. Test max once a month, not weekly. If you're sore, hurting, or weaker two sessions in a row, cut volume before adding more.

Frequency is three dials, not one

"How often" gets asked as if there's a single number. There isn't. Your weekly dose is the product of three dials:

  • Sessions per week — how many days you touch the bar.
  • Holds per session — how many sets, and how long each one lasts.
  • Intensity — how close each hold is to your true max, or how much added weight you're using.

Two sessions of five hard holds and five sessions of one easy hold are both "five hangs a week," but they're not the same stimulus. Most people who ask about frequency are really asking one of three questions: What's the minimum I can do and still improve? How do I stop feeling beat up? How do I get to a pull-up fastest? The answer changes with the goal.

What the evidence actually supports

There is no dead-hang-specific randomized trial that answers "how many days per week should I hang?" Anyone who tells you otherwise is extrapolating. What we do have is solid general resistance-training evidence:

  • A 2018 meta-analysis of resistance-training frequency found that, when total volume is equated, training a muscle group 1–3 times per week produces broadly similar strength gains; the practical takeaway is that frequency is a tool for distributing volume, not a magic multiplier [1].
  • The 2026 ACSM position stand on resistance training prescription recommends roughly 2–3 sessions per week for beginners and intermediate trainees, with progressive overload and adequate recovery [2].
  • Dead hangs are isometric grip/shoulder work, not a maximal strength lift. That makes them relatively low-fatigue per set — which is why daily easy hangs can coexist with harder sessions, as long as you're not chasing failure every day.

The honest summary: 2–3 hard-ish sessions per week is a well-supported starting point; daily easy hangs are a habit tool, not a strength program. If you're new, start at the low end and add sessions before you add intensity.

The 2-minute test (once a month, not weekly)

Frequency without measurement is just guessing. Once a month, test your max hang under identical conditions: same bar, same grip (use overhand unless you have a reason not to), same time of day, no fresh chalk if you don't normally use it. Warm up with two easy hangs, rest three minutes, then hang until you have to let go. Log it. Use the dead hang calculator to see where that number lands for your age and sex, and the progression planner to turn it into a block.

Why monthly? A weekly max test adds fatigue and noise without adding information. Your day-to-day grip fluctuates with sleep, stress, and what you did yesterday. Monthly is frequent enough to see a trend and rare enough to not become its own training stress.

If you're doing the first pull-up progression, keep the hangs on the same three days and add scapular pulls and negative pull-ups after them. If you're over 50 or returning from a layoff, the over-50 guide starts more conservatively.

Signs you're doing too much

  • Finger or palm pain that lasts more than a day — especially at the base of the fingers or the elbow. Back off; don't "push through" tendon pain.
  • Shoulder pain that changes your sleep or overhead reach — stop the hangs and get it assessed if it persists.
  • Performance drops for two sessions in a row — that's fatigue, not a need for more volume.
  • You're maxing every session — that's a test, not training. It will stall you.

This is general training guidance, not medical advice. If you have an injury, a spine condition, or you're returning post-surgery, get clearance first.

Signs you're doing too little

  • Four weeks pass with no change in max hang or comfort.
  • You only hang once a week, and only when you remember.
  • You never get within 5 seconds of your limit, so there's no stimulus to adapt.

If that's you, add one session before adding weight or intensity. Consistency is the cheapest variable to change.

Common mistakes

  1. Maxing daily. It feels productive and stalls progress. Test monthly.
  2. Adding weight before 60 seconds. Build a clean bodyweight base first; the muscle guide explains what's actually adapting.
  3. Program hopping every two weeks. Give a dose 3–4 weeks before judging it.
  4. Counting a 5-second hang as a session. Fine for habit, not for strength.
  5. Ignoring pain because "hanging is natural." Natural doesn't mean appropriate for every shoulder.

FAQ

Can I dead hang every day?

Yes, if the hangs are easy and short — 1–2 holds of 10–20 seconds, never near max. Daily maxing is not recommended; keep hard sessions to 2–3 per week.

Is 2 times a week enough?

For general grip and shoulder maintenance, it can be. For a first pull-up or measurable strength gain, 3 sessions is a better starting point.

Should I hang before or after a workout?

Either, as long as you're consistent. If grip is the priority, do hangs before grip-heavy pulling; if pull-ups are the priority, do them first and hang after.

How long should each session take?

Most people get the stimulus in 5–10 minutes. If a session is taking 30 minutes, you're probably resting too long or doing too many sets.

How often should I test my max?

Once a month, under the same conditions. Weekly testing adds fatigue and noise.
