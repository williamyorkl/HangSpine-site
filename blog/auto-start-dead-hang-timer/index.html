Auto-start dead hang timers

You grab the bar; the timer is already running. You let go; it stops and logs the set. Auto-start sounds like a small luxury until you remember that hanging is the one exercise where your hands are — by definition — unavailable. Here's how the detection actually works, who does it well, and what it quietly changes about your training.

By William, developer of HangSpine (one of the apps below — disclosed throughout) · Nov 5, 2026
TL;DR + DISCLOSURE

Auto-start hang timers use the Apple Watch's motion sensors to detect mounting the bar and begin timing with zero taps — the feature category is new, small, and genuinely useful. On watchOS, the honest field: HangSpine (ours — gyroscope mount/dismount detection, on-device learning), Hang Habit (tap-to-arm + motion detection), and hangtime ("No buttons. Just hang."), plus manual-timer apps for everyone else. We make one of these, so every claim below is sourced or marked as ours. If you're new to hanging itself, start with the benchmarks calculator and the progression planner.

The problem auto-start actually solves

Every manual timing path fails at the same moment: the start. Phone on the floor, timer on the wrist — either way, firing it demands a tap, and hanging is the one exercise where both hands are committed, overhead, under your full bodyweight, with forearms you are actively exhausting. People solve this creatively (chin-to-screen taps, pre-started stopwatches, asking the dog), and every workaround adds the same thing: a little friction between deciding to hang and hanging. Enough of those little frictions and the daily habit quietly dies — which is the real stakes here, because hanging's benefits compound on consistency, not heroics.

Auto-start deletes the moment entirely. Motion sensors notice the mount; timing begins; sensors notice the release; timing stops and the set is logged. No buttons exist in the workflow. That last part — logged — matters more than people expect: complete data without bookkeeping, which is what makes streaks, totals and trends real instead of approximate.

How the watch knows you're hanging

There's no magic, just physics wearing a wristband. Three signals do the work:

  1. Wrist orientation. A raised, overhead, loaded arm produces a distinctive gravity-vector pattern on the watch's sensors — the "you are hanging-shaped" signature.
  2. Mount dynamics. The swing-and-settle of stepping up to the bar looks nothing like a wave or a stretch; the transition is recognizable.
  3. Real grip tremor. Under actual bodyweight load, forearms carry a fine physiological tremor that an empty-hand raise can't imitate — the difference between hanging and pretending (we open our detection traces so you can see your own).

The engineering question is how each app uses those signals. Fixed thresholds fit nobody perfectly — bar heights, arm lengths and mount styles all differ — so the better approach is learning your signature. HangSpine, for instance, records each session's motion trace and re-computes its detection parameters on-device over time, so week six recognizes you better than day one. (That's our approach, disclosed; competitors take their own.)

When detection gets it wrong (and how good apps cope)

Honest engineering note: auto-start isn't clairvoyant. The classic false-positive is adjusting your grip — a re-grip mid-set can look, to naive thresholds, like a dismount-and-remount, splitting one long hang into two short ones. Swinging kip-style, wiping chalk, or a partner jostling the bar each produce odd signatures too. The defenses differ by app: confirmation windows (a release must persist for a beat before the set closes), grip-energy checks that distinguish loaded hands from relaxed ones, and — the approach we take — per-person learning that calibrates to your re-grip habit after a few sessions. The practical takeaway for users: your first week with any auto-detecting app is its calibration week, so glance at the session summary and, if an app offers feedback buttons, use them. Good detection is a conversation, not a purchase.

What auto-start changes in practice

  • The "walk-by hang" becomes real. Passing the bar? Ten seconds costs nothing — no arming ritual. Micro-sessions sum into real weekly volume, and volume is the training variable that moves hang numbers (see the planner).
  • Data stops flattering you. A stopwatch estimates; sensors measure. Your max, your totals, your trends — trustworthy enough to train by.
  • The habit gets an honest scoreboard. Streaks only motivate when they're real, and they're only real when every set is counted automatically.
  • Test days stay pure. One honest max attempt a month, logged by the same system that logged the easy weeks — no stopwatch optimism.

Buying advice, including against ourselves

If you own an Apple Watch and hang even twice a week, an auto-start timer of any of the three flavors above will improve your consistency; pick on price and interface taste. If you hang rarely and casually, a free web timer costs nothing. If you're a Garmin owner, Dead Hang Pro's drop-detection is the nearest neighbor on that platform. And if you mainly want to know where you stand before buying anything at all: our dead hang calculator benchmarks your max in thirty seconds, free, no signup — that part we're certain you should do regardless.

FAQ

What is an auto-start dead hang timer?

A timer that begins counting when sensors detect you've mounted the bar and stops at release — no button press at either end, every set logged automatically.

Which apps truly auto-start?

On watchOS: HangSpine (full auto + on-device learning), Hang Habit (tap-to-arm + motion detection), hangtime (automatic). Most other hang timers are manual.

How does the detection work?

Wrist orientation + mount dynamics + load tremor; the best implementations learn your personal pattern rather than using fixed thresholds.

Gimmick or game-changer?

It removes the exact friction that kills hanging habits, and it makes data complete by default. Small feature, outsized consistency effect.
